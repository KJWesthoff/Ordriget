# The learning design and why

## Who it's for

He is a native Danish *speaker* who learned to *read and write* in English (TK through 5th grade). His spoken
Danish is fine. What needs work is mapping Danish sounds to Danish letters, where his instinct is the English
spelling system. That leads to two kinds of errors:

1. **Danish-internal traps.** These are the same ones Danish children struggle with, because Danish spelling is
   one of the least sound-predictable in Europe. A 2005 study of Danish 4th–6th graders found vowels and double
   consonants to be the main sources of error, with predictability of the spelling strongly affecting
   performance (Juul).
2. **Cross-language transfer.** He reaches for an English letter pattern (c/k, w/v, ph/f, x/ks, sh, oo, -y). Studies
   of bilingual children (mostly Spanish–English) show exactly this: children spell contrastive vowels and
   patterns using the rules of the language they learned to write first.

## How the game uses this

| Area | Focus | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Engdalen | e / æ / i | Many words *sound* like æ but are spelled e (hest, seks); the "a-family" tip (mand → mænd) |
| Skyggeskoven | ø / y / u | Short y sounds like ø (bygge, kys), short u sounds like o (hund). Neither ø nor y-as-vowel exists in English |
| Kysten | å / o | Short o sounds like å (sove, ost, godt) |
| Bjergene | silent letters | hv-, hj-, d after n/l/r, silent g/v |
| Isslottet | present-tense -r, double consonants | The classic "nutids-r" (lærer/lære), using Dansk Sprognævn's swap test ("spiser"), plus kat → katten |
| Vrøvls Tårn | English traps | k not c, v not w, f not ph, ks not x, sk/sj not sh, -ie not -y, plus the exceptions (cykel, cirkus) |

Every word also has its English equivalent, shown as a **bridge** (`EN sister → søster`) when he makes a
mistake. This is contrastive analysis: the difference between the two languages is named explicitly instead of
left for him to discover.

## Research-backed mechanics

- **Retrieval practice with immediate corrective feedback.** Spelling a word from memory beats copying or
  "rainbow writing", and in a study of 5th graders the benefit held after 4 days, but *only* when immediate
  corrective feedback was given. So every wrong answer is followed by the correct word with the tricky letters
  highlighted, a rule tip, and the English bridge.
- **Se – Dæk – Skriv – Tjek (Look, Cover, Write, Check).** After a mistake he has to look at the word, then it is
  hidden and he types it from memory. That's a second retrieval, not a copy.
- **Spaced repetition (Leitner boxes).** Each word moves up a box when he spells it correctly without a hint, and
  then comes back after 1, 3, 7, 14 and 30 days. A miss sends it back to box 0. Missed words also return 3
  questions later in the same battle (expanding retrieval). Spacing and retrieval reliably improve learning and
  work even better combined.
- **Morphology and patterns over word lists.** Danish children above the earliest stages spell using units
  larger than single sounds, and direct training in fixed spelling patterns helped 3rd graders spell *new*
  words. So each area teaches one pattern, and the tips explain the rule (a-family, hv-question words, the
  "spiser" test, short vowel + more letters → double consonant).
- **Dictation (diktat).** With a Danish voice installed, the magic attacks read the word and a sentence aloud, and
  he spells the whole word. This is the strongest exercise, and it's rewarded with the most damage.
- **Difficulty ladder.** Choosing a letter (sword) → choosing the correct spelling → spelling the whole word
  (fire) → correcting a misspelling (lightning). Easier modes build confidence, and the harder ones carry
  bigger rewards.
- **No time pressure.** Speed isn't rewarded, only accuracy and streaks. Losing a battle keeps all XP earned.
- **Little and often.** The daily treasure chest in the Training Tower rewards a short session every day.

## Suggestions outside the game

- Put his weekly school spelling list into **Egne ord** (custom words).
- Have him read Danish books aloud, then occasionally dictate a sentence from the page.
- When he writes, praise the English-to-Danish "translation" as a skill ("you knew it was *kage*, not *cake*").
  The game's story is built around the same message: two languages are a superpower, not a weakness.
